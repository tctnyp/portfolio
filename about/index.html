About Me

How bypassing a school Chromebook turned into running servers.

This isn't a CV. It's the long version — the detours, the bad ideas, and the moments where a small annoyance turned into something I genuinely care about.

  • Tan Chee Tiong
  • Singapore
  • Hosting · Programming · Minecraft

Hey, everyone. I'm Tan Chee Tiong — an aspiring developer with a stubborn interest in server hosting, programming, and building Minecraft servers that people actually want to play on. If you only read one line of this page, make it this one: almost everything I know started because something in front of me was broken, slow, or locked down, and I refused to leave it alone.

I've written this page as a set of chapters rather than a bullet list, because the bullet list version wouldn't make much sense. “Runs Minecraft networks” sounds random until you know it started with a school laptop I wasn't supposed to modify. The path from there to here was messy, and the messy part is the interesting part.

So: four years, one Chromebook, several servers, and a lot of things I broke before I understood them.

2022 — Secondary 1

The Chromebook that started everything

In Secondary 1, the school gave us Chromebooks to use for our studies. On paper it was a good idea. In practice, mine was slow, heavily managed, and restricted in ways that felt completely arbitrary. Sometimes it would block the most random sites for no reason at all — documentation pages, reference material, things I actually needed. That got annoying very quickly, and I started looking for a way around it.

The internet, predictably, had opinions. The first method I tried was repeatedly disabling the management extension using scripts. Another was corrupting the extension's installation so it couldn't load at all. Both worked — for a while. Both were temporary. The extension would eventually repair itself, sync back down from the management server, and everything I'd done would quietly undo itself overnight.

What I didn't realise at the time was that the failures were the useful part. To make any of it work I had to understand what an extension actually is, how enterprise enrollment pushes policy to a device, why a managed machine phones home, and what a “managed” device really means when someone else holds the keys. I wasn't studying any of that. I was just trying to open a website. I learned it anyway.

Somewhere in that rabbit hole I found a way to permanently unenroll the Chromebook. I sat on it for months. I knew it would work, and I knew what would happen if it went wrong, so I did nothing — for a whole year.

[2023] Secondary 2

The Unenrollment

Server hosting pushed me to think about reliability, uptime, maintenance, and the quiet details people only notice when something fails. It taught me to design for stability, not just for the first successful launch.

That mindset changed how I look at every project. I now care a lot about whether something stays understandable after I come back to it later, not just whether it works once.

Chapter Three

Building with purpose.

I focus on code and infrastructure that are understandable, maintainable, and useful for real people. That includes keeping workflows clear, reducing unnecessary complexity, and making sure the final result feels calm to use.

I'm especially interested in the overlap between programming, hosting, and Minecraft development, because that combination lets me build systems that are both technical and practical.
